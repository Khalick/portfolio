Every good business in your town

Africa’s best local businesses are invisible online. Fixing that is less a technology problem than a trust problem.

Think of the best mechanic in your town. The one who tells you the truth about your car, charges fairly and finishes when they say they will. Now try to find them online.

You probably can’t. You found them the way everyone in Kenya finds a good business: someone told you. A neighbour, a cousin, a colleague. Word of mouth is our real search engine, and it has served us well for generations. But it has a hard limit. It only works if you already know the right person.

Move to a new town, travel for work, or just need something you’ve never needed before, and word of mouth stops working. The good businesses are still there. You just can’t see them.

The invisible majority

Most of the economy in African towns runs on small businesses: food vendors, salons, hardware shops, tailors, mechanics, pharmacies. They are the economy most people actually live in every day. Yet online they barely exist. The global platforms were built for a different market. They assume a business already has a website, a marketing budget and someone whose job is managing its online reviews.

The woman running the best kibanda in town has none of those. She has a phone, a loyal set of customers and a reputation she earned one plate at a time. That reputation is valuable, and right now it is trapped inside the heads of the people who already know her.

Listings aren’t the hard part

Building a directory is easy. Anyone can scrape a list of names and phone numbers and call it a marketplace. The internet is full of directories nobody uses, because nobody trusts them. Half the numbers are dead, some of the businesses have closed, and the reviews might have been written by the owner’s friends.

So the real problem isn’t visibility. It’s trusted visibility. A customer has to believe that if a business appears on the platform, it is real, it is open, and the people reviewing it actually went there.

What we decided at Lokari

When we built Lokari, we made three decisions that shape everything else.

Every business is checked by a person before it goes live. Not an algorithm, a person. It is slower and it costs us more, and that is the point. A marketplace is only worth as much as its weakest listing.

Listing is free, and stays free. The businesses that most need to be found are often the ones least able to pay for it. If we charge for a spot at the table, we rebuild the same exclusion that made them invisible in the first place.

We serve both sides. Lokari is B2B2C. Businesses get the tools to be discovered: a verified profile, deals, events and reels. Customers get one place they can trust to find what’s nearby, filtered by town, category and rating. Neither side works without the other.

Where this goes

Lokari now lists businesses across 57 towns in Kenya. That’s a start, not a finish. The goal is simple to describe and hard to build: wherever you are, in any town, you should be able to find every good business near you and trust what you find.

Word of mouth got us this far. Our job is to make it work for everyone, not just the people who already know someone.

Peter Agak Odongo is the founder of Lokari Africa and SoulScript.
