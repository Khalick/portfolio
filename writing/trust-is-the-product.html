Trust is the product

In a marketplace, features are table stakes. What you are really building is trust, and it doesn’t scale the way code does.

Engineers like me are trained to love things that scale. Write the code once and it serves a million people. That instinct is mostly right, and it is the reason software changes the world.

But building a marketplace taught me where the instinct breaks. Some of the most valuable work in a two-sided business doesn’t scale at all, at least not at first. And trying to skip it is how most marketplaces die.

Every marketplace sells the same thing

On the surface a marketplace sells listings, search, reviews and deals. Underneath, it sells one thing: the confidence to act. A customer who finds a business on the platform has to feel safe calling them, walking in, or paying. A business that lists has to believe the customers who arrive are real and the reviews are fair.

Take that confidence away and the features are worthless. A beautiful search page full of dead numbers is worse than no search page at all. It teaches people not to come back.

The unscalable part

At Lokari, every business is checked by a real person before it goes live. People sometimes ask why we don’t automate it. The honest answer is that early on, the manual work is the product. It’s how we learn what a fake listing looks like, which details customers actually care about, and what a good business in a small town looks like compared with one in Nairobi.

Automation comes later, built from what the manual work taught us. If you automate before you understand, you just make the same mistakes faster.

Reviews you can believe

The same logic applies to reviews. An anonymous star rating from someone who may never have visited is noise. So Lokari leans on verified visits: a customer shares a photo and a short review of an actual visit. It’s a little more effort for the reviewer. That effort is the signal.

Free is a trust decision too

Listing on Lokari is free, and that is also about trust. If businesses could pay to appear, customers would rightly wonder whether they were seeing the best businesses or just the ones with the biggest budgets. Keeping the door open to every good business, whatever its size, keeps the results honest.

What I’d tell another founder

If you’re building a marketplace in Africa, or anywhere, don’t ask “what features do we need?” first. Ask “why would a stranger trust what they see here?” Then do whatever it takes to make that answer true, even if it doesn’t scale yet.

Features can be copied in a weekend. Trust takes years to build, and that is exactly why it lasts.
